--- Introduction ---
Retirement is contrary to classic FP&A, which focuses on accumulation of funds and the growth of a portfolio. Rather than accumulation,
retirement is focused on decumulation. The main problem with decumulation is Sequence of Returns Risk (SORR) Sequence of return risk 
refers to the risk of receiving poor investment returns early in retirement when withdrawals are being made from a portfolio. If you 
experience a market crash early in your retirement, you are forced to sell off a larger percentage of your portfolio to generate your 
required cash. Those sold shares can never benefit from the eventual market recovery. Therefore, two portfolios with the exact same 
average return over 30 years can have drastically different outcomes depending on the order in which those returns occurred.

To attempt to model this, we are unable to use deterministic "average return" methods, as they do not attempt to account for the numerous
possible ways that losses can be incurred in the future. The market has nearly endless possible paths to profit as well as financial ruin
and they cannot be simplified out when considering retirement calculations exactly because of SORR, and its unpredictable nature. We must
use a discrete-time stochastic process. For each time step $t$ (representing one year), the portfolio value $V_t$ is a function of the 
previous year's value $V_{t-1}$, a random market return $R_{t}$, and the required withdrawal amount $W_{t}$. This is given by the
function:

$$V_{t} = (V_{t-1} \times (1 + R_t)) - W_t$$

Consider the equation as such: Take an initial amount, the stock market will either grow or shrink it, then take out living expenses. The
remaining balance is the balance going into the next FY. The issues in calculations arise due to the volatility of the market as well as 
life in general (living expenses may spike due to unforeseen circumstances, macro events majorly impact the market, etc.) Because the 
market is unpredictable, $R_t$ is a random variable. We assume logarithmic returns are normally distributed. Therefore, we sample $R_t$ 
from a Gaussian distribution:

$$R_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$$

(Where $\mu$ is the expected market return and $\sigma$ is the market volatility. This notation denotes a normal distribution with mean
mu and variance of sigma squared.)

Because $R_t$ is randomly distributed, there is no way to calculate the final portfolio value in a definite way. SORR dictates that the 
order of the random numbers and outcomes are supremely important, even more so than the numbers themselves. We must plan for as many paths
as we can, and try to consider an average outcome to plan out future steps to avoid financial ruin in the portfolio. In order to do so,
we use a Monte Carlo simulation. Monte Carlo relies on the Law of Large Numbers and empirical probability. By simulating thousands of 
possible paths using our stochastic equation, we generate a massive dataset of potential outcomes. We can then calculate the empirical 
probability of ruin (e.g., if 1,500 out of 10,000 paths fall below $0, the probability of ruin is 15%).

--- Single Path Calculation ---
- calc_single_path
takes an initial portfolio value, an annual withdrawal amount, an inflation rate (approx), a mean and std for market returns, and a numbers
of years to simulate over. the mean and std of market returns can be found online, but it is calculated based on historical data, so any
catastrophic events may not be well simulated here, but for the sake of the simulation, we will assume there will be no imminent apocalypse
port_history: initialize list for the annual value of the portfolio, starting with 0
curr_val: starts at the initial portfolio value, then based on the V_t equation from above, calculates the new portfolio value based on the
market return (multiplied first) then the subtraction from withdrawals. ** market operations compound THEN withdrawals are made (order)
curr_withdraw: annual portfolio withdrawal, set to a constant for this
for each year in the given range, the market return is first calcualted using np.random.normal, which draws random samples from a Gaussian
distribution. It takes a loc (“centre” of the distribution), and a scale ("spread" of the distribution) as arguments. these are given by the
input to the overarching function. this means for each iteration, there will be a random market return, somewhat simulating the randomness of 
the real market, but of course this is simplified as well. 
after the change in portfolio current value occurs, then the loop checks if the value frops below 0. this sets the portfolio to 0, "flatlining"
then the new portfolio value is logged in the port_hist list, and then the withdrawal for the next year is set by the current withdrawal, which
is adjusted by the inflation rate given as an input. This does present another assumption that the inflation rate will hold steady for the 
observed years of the portfolio.
finally, the list of historical portfolio values is returned.

--- Monte Carlo --- 
One simplified path is not enough in order to make a claim or predict how a portfolio will perform due to the complexity of SORR. In order to 
remedy this, we use a Monte Carlo simulation, where we simulate 10000 paths for 30 years, to find the likely states of a portfolio after market
changes. (Probabilistic outcomes are uncertain, potential results of actions or events, defined by their likelihood rather than certainty.)
Essentially, we build a probability density function for the unknown futures, such that we may try to find a most likely range of outcomes. 

In simple Monte Carlo simulations, a for loop may do the job, but for this, due to the sheer volume of calculations, I call upon Numpy for 
vectorized operations. We also use the time library to see time taken for execution (just out of curiosity)

- run_monte_carlo
we essentialy perform the same calculations in the single path calculation, but in bulk by defining the operations that must be perfomed on a 
table of 10000 x 30 (matrix) of simulations over years. 
np.zeros: set up blank table, with n+1 rows to hold Year 0, num_sims columns for each individual simulation. 
ret_matrix: preset the distributed means and sds of the markets for each year of each simulation, preventing 300k calculations, one per loop
new_vals:prev_vals and mkt_rets_this_yr are np arrays of 10,000 numbers, basic math operators (*, +, -) automatically apply to every single 
item in the array simultaneously.
np.maximum: vectorized equivalent of our if curr_val < 0, compares 2 arrays: 1 of the portfolio values, one of all 0s, and replaces the lower.

--- Visualization ---
Now that we have a functional Monte Carlo engine, I want to actually see how the values change rather than have the black box of the function,
and only see the sample outputs that are called in the terminal by the test. the two implemented charts that would provide some relevant insight
into portfolio performance are a spaghetti plot of a set of paths, and a histogram of final value performance. we do fig with ax1 and ax2 using 
subplot. 

- spaghetti plot: a data visualization tool displaying numerous individual lines together on one graph. this is useful for seeing the trajectories
of the displayed portfolios, to get a better idea of the average performance of the portfolio, as well as the behavior of outliers. if a large 
spread of lines is seen by the 30th year of simulation, then this supports the SORR theory that there is unpredictable market risk in portfolio 
growth and withdrawals. 
ax1 plot slices the portfolio matrix to include the first 500 simulated portfolios to be visualized. the alpha is set to 0.05 (opacity of lines)
in order to see the trend better with such a large collection of lines. there is an anonymous (lambda) function that cleans up the values into 
readable strings for the user on the axis.  

- histogram : The histogram shows a distribution of final performances of the portfolio. this can allow us to approximate the performance of 
portfolios to a known or analogous distribution, giving insight into the behavior of risk.
this plot takes all the data, but seperates it into 50 bins, using the same lambda function in order to format the text on axis.
there is a line drawn at $0 in order to denote portfolios that have failed or run dry by the end of the time frame.

--- Risk Metrics --- 
Analysis via Pandas. Now that we have simulated the portfolio, we can run analysis on any one of the paths.
- calc_risk_metrics:
we can call a specific year to target in this function. target_yr_values is the specific row for the index called.
df_vals will be the conversion of a NumPy array into a Pandas Series for data analysis. given this, we can calculate the probability of ruin.
we take the number of ruined portfolios (the portfolios wehere the series has the target value of 0), and sums them together (count)
then the total number of sims is simply calculated by taking the length of the number of indexes in the portfolio matrix
finally, the probability is calculated by x/n, and then multiplied by 100 to give a percentage.
then pd.quantiles are created. verages (means) are incredibly dangerous in finance because extreme outliers warp them. If 9,999 people go broke 
and 1 person makes a trillion dollars, the average outcome looks great. Quantiles sort the 10,000 paths from worst to best. The 5th quantile 
(0.05) looks at the exact portfolio sitting at position 500. Translated: 95% of the time, you will have more money than this.
best outcome is printed as well, just so you can see the lucky winner of the simulation :0
